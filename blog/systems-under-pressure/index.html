What twenty years taught me about systems

Stuart White Updated September 22, 2026 3 min read

A delivery deadline slips. The last team in the chain gets the difficult conversation. But the decision that made the delay inevitable may have happened weeks earlier, in another department, before anyone thought there was a problem.

My twenty years of experience span Zipper Interactive, Turn 10 Studios, Microsoft, 343 Industries, Bungie, and EA. Working across quality, production, and interdependent teams taught me to look beyond the visible failure. That habit now informs my independent consulting work at CouloirGG.

Trace the cascade

A requirement changes after planning. A dependency arrives late. Testing starts with less time than it needs. The release absorbs the cost. Each team can be doing reasonable work while the overall process produces a result nobody wanted.

The useful question is where the first assumption stopped being true. What did each team expect to receive? What actually arrived? Who knew the difference, and where could they send that information?

The place where a problem becomes visible is often the place absorbing it.

When an organisation says delivery keeps missing deadlines, I start with the handoffs. Asking the delivery team to move faster will not fix missing requirements or decisions that arrive after the work has started.

Protect the work already running

Operating a service while improving it creates competing demands on the same people. An incident interrupts a planned change. The change introduces another dependency. Work that looked manageable on a roadmap becomes a series of urgent exceptions.

The response starts with visibility and ownership: who handles interruptions, how capacity changes when they happen, and which commitments need to be revisited. Without that structure, individuals make the tradeoffs privately and the cost appears somewhere else.

A useful process gives people a way to make those decisions together, with enough context to understand what they are postponing.

Listen across the handoffs

People in quality, coordination, support, and release management see the same failure pass through multiple teams. Their work gives them a perspective that an individual team's dashboard may never show.

I start by listening to those people. Which information do they keep asking for? What do they copy between tools? Which workarounds would stop the process if they stopped doing them?

A workaround is evidence of a gap the formal process has not addressed.

Those conversations are often enough to narrow a broad complaint into a specific intervention: a clearer owner, a connection between two systems, or a decision recorded where the next team can find it.

Adapt the method

The experience transfers. The exact workflow usually does not. An organisation with a monthly delivery cycle needs different controls from one handling changes throughout the day.

I map the actual operation before proposing a fix. The goal is to remove recurring friction while preserving the parts people already rely on. That means understanding the work, building a targeted change, and checking whether it helps the team in practice.

Some of the most useful improvements address a problem people have stopped noticing. A manual handoff repeated every morning may never trigger a crisis. It can still consume time and lose context each time it happens.

If delays keep spreading between your teams, I can help trace the dependencies and agree where to start.
