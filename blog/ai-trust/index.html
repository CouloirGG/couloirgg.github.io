Building AI tools people can trust

Stuart White Updated September 24, 2026 3 min read

Knowing a conversation is being recorded can change what someone is willing to say. AI adds another question: who will see the summary, and what will they think it means?

That is a practical concern when helping a team adopt AI tools. Useful notes depend on people understanding what is captured, where it is processed, and how it will be shared.

Make consent understandable

Before recording starts, participants should know what will be captured and who will have access. The person running the session needs to obtain their consent and respect a decision not to record.

A tool may ask its user to acknowledge a consent notice before starting a session. That acknowledgement does not establish that everyone present has agreed. The person running the session still needs to obtain participant consent.

I want the process around the tool to make those responsibilities clear. A recording indicator helps people know a session is active; it cannot replace the conversation about whether it should be recorded.

Explain where the data goes

Some tools support local transcription and analysis, allowing a session to be processed on the team's own hardware. That can be useful when conversations need to stay within a controlled environment.

Cloud transcription or AI analysis sends relevant audio or text to an external provider. Sharing notes with a team introduces another choice about who can access the output. Check how the chosen tool handles each step.

Those options need clear explanations. Calling an application local-first should not leave someone assuming that every configuration keeps all data on the device.

People should be able to explain where their recording goes before they start one.

Account for the trade-offs

Local processing depends on the available hardware and models. Cloud processing introduces another provider and its data-handling terms. Asking for consent takes time, and some sessions should remain unrecorded.

These are practical choices to make with the team. The right configuration depends on the work, the people involved, and the information being discussed.

A tool also needs a useful path when recording is not appropriate. A brief written decision or an agreed action list may be enough for that session.

Keep people in the review

An AI summary is an interpretation of a conversation. It can miss context, attribute an action incorrectly, or present an open question as a decision. A fluent report still needs checking.

In my view, trust depends on making that review part of the workflow. The people responsible for an action should be able to check what was captured and clarify what they agreed to do.

The aim is useful context that people understand and can work with. The amount of audio recorded is a poor measure of that on its own.
